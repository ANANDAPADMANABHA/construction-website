When you start designing a home, you'll hear about "2D plans" and "3D elevations."
They sound like alternatives — pick one — but they're actually two different tools
that answer two different questions. Here's how they differ and why you usually
need both.

## What a 2D plan is

A **2D plan** is the top-down view of your home — the floor plan. It shows:

- Room sizes and positions
- How spaces connect and how you move through the home
- Door and window positions
- Wall layout and dimensions

The 2D plan is about **function**: does the home work for how your family lives?
Is there enough light and ventilation? Does the kitchen relate well to the dining
area? This is the most important design decision you'll make, because layout is
what you live in every day. See our [2D house plan service](/services/2d-house-plans).

## What a 3D elevation is

A **3D elevation** is a realistic view of the **exterior** of your home — the
facade. It shows:

- How the front (and other sides) will actually look
- Materials, colours and textures
- The overall style — contemporary, traditional Kerala, minimal

The 3D elevation is about **appearance**: how your home presents itself to the
world. Because it's the hardest thing to change once built, seeing it in 3D first
is genuinely valuable. See our [3D elevation service](/services/3d-elevation-design).

## Why you usually need both

| | 2D Plan | 3D Elevation |
|---|---|---|
| Answers | How does it work to live in? | How does it look? |
| View | Top-down layout | Realistic exterior |
| Decides | Function, flow, light | Facade, style, materials |

The two work together. The 3D elevation is modelled **on top of** the 2D plan —
the layout determines the massing that the elevation then dresses. That's why the
order matters.

## The right order

1. **2D plan first** — settle the layout, room sizes, light and flow.
2. **3D elevation next** — design the exterior on the finalised plan.
3. **Then permit and construction** — with both design decisions locked in.

Getting them in this order avoids expensive rework, because you're not
redesigning a facade for a layout that keeps changing.
